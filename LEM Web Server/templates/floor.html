Rendering quality

Remembered on this screen only. Automatic measures the frame and steps itself down when it has to; pick a level to stop it moving. Lower levels drop roughness maps and texture resolution first — the forest keeps its extent at every level, because a thinner world is a worse answer than a slightly softer one.

Render resolution

Separate from quality, and your choice. Quality decides how much work a frame is — shadows, lighting, vegetation. Resolution decides how sharp it is. They used to be bundled, so asking for a cheaper level also quietly dropped the resolution: Floor was rendering 36% of the pixels on your screen and stretching them back up. Full now means your screen's real resolution, not a scaled one — on a Retina display that is twice the pixels in each direction.

One thing this cannot sharpen: each quality level also loads its textures at a different size, and that is decided when the world is built — Ultra 100%, High 75%, Medium 50%, Low 35%, Floor 25%. So Floor at Full resolution draws crisp geometry with quarter-size surfaces on it. Changing quality here re-applies the lighting immediately; the textures follow on the next reload.

Frame cadence

If the site looks like it is hitching even though the frame rate seems fine, this is usually why. When a frame costs slightly more than one refresh of your monitor, the browser alternates between showing it after one refresh and after two — measured here, 55% of frames arrived after one and 41% after two. The average looks healthy and the motion stutters. Chrome shows this more readily than Safari does. Holding a slower but steady cadence fixes it: capping to 60 took the frame-to-frame jitter down by three and a half times.

Correction factors —

Added to the raw reading of every measurement this piece of equipment reports — customer samples as well as QC: corrected = raw + correction. The corrected value is what gets reported; the raw reading is kept in the record. Leave at 0 for no correction. The bench picks the change up on its next poll.
